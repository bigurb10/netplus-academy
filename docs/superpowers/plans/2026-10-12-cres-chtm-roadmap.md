# CRES and CHTM roadmap (AAMI/ACI, 2026 handbook)

**Goal:** Record what a CRES Academy pack and a CHTM Academy pack would contain, so that when we decide to build them the exam facts, the draft outlines, the reuse from the CBET pack, and the reference bill are already settled.

**Status:** Planning only. Nothing is built, no seed materials are bought, no branch exists. Source of truth for every number below is the *ACI Certification Candidate Handbook 2026* (`E:\CERT GUIDES\BMET\ACI-Certification-Handbook-090926.pdf`): CRES on printed pages 15 to 19, CHTM on 20 to 24, exam windows on 31, fees on 33, results and retakes on 37 to 38, references on 40 to 44.

## 1. Purpose and status

This note is a planning document, not a plan to execute. Two gates come first:

1. **BMET Academy ships.** Plan A (engine) is merged; Plan B (the content pack) is in flight. Nothing new starts until `courses/bmet/` is live and the catalog's "Start here" section is in front of real visitors.
2. **The first CBET feedback arrives.** CBET Academy is the closest neighbour of both credentials. The in-course feedback buttons and the weekly email pipeline will tell us whether a 60-lesson AAMI pack converts at all before we write a second and a third.

No seed materials have been bought for either credential. That matters more than it sounds: the house rule is that a pack is not done until every term is checked against reference books on disk, and the only books on disk today are the ACI handbook and *Electronics For Dummies*. Buying the three radiology books is therefore the first real task of a CRES build, not a later step.

Both credentials are a step up from CBET rather than sideways from it. CRES is the same technician job in the imaging department. CHTM is the manager above that technician, and almost none of its content is bench work.

## 2. CRES: Certified Radiology Equipment Specialist

### Scope (handbook page 16)

The handbook expects experience across a wide range of medical imaging modalities plus the electromechanical devices, computers, networks and software used to deliver care. Candidates work for device manufacturers, hospitals, clinics, repair companies, regulatory bodies, and software makers such as EMR or device integration providers. The duty list is the CBET duty list aimed at imaging: PM and calibration, corrective maintenance in clinical, bench or depot settings, regulatory compliance (CMS, FDA GMP), managing medical software and hardware systems such as PACS administration and integration, educating users, reviewing technical manuals, documenting maintenance, and troubleshooting medical device networks.

Audience in plain words: the imaging technician, or the CBET-level technician who has moved into the radiology shop.

### Eligibility (handbook page 17)

A CRES candidate applies for either full status or candidate status.

**Full certification.** ONE of these three pathways, before the application deadline:

| # | Pathway |
|---|---|
| 1 | Associate degree or higher in an electronics, technology or science program, plus two years full-time BMET work experience |
| 2 | Completion of a U.S. military biomedical equipment technology program, plus two years full-time BMET work experience |
| 3 | Four years full-time BMET work experience |

**And, in addition to whichever pathway applies:** at least 40 percent of the applicant's work experience must be in the radiology specialty area over the last two years, with 25 percent over the last five years. This clause is the thing that stops a general CBET from simply sitting the exam, and any marketing copy we write has to say it plainly.

**Candidate status.** An applicant who wants full certification but does not yet qualify may apply through candidate status with ONE of: an associate degree or higher in an electronics, technology or science program; completion of a U.S. military biomedical equipment technology program; or two years of full-time BMET work experience. Passing candidates get a letter confirming "candidate" status and have **five years** to meet the full requirements, then file the Certification Upgrade Form and pay a small fee for the application and exam to be reviewed again. Until the upgrade is approved the person is not a certificant and may not use the designation.

### Exam modality and fees

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Offered | Twice a year, May and November; the windows are May 1 to 15 and November 1 to 15 (page 31) |
| Delivery | Computer-based testing at designated test centers, currently Kryterion; travel of up to 100 miles may be needed |
| Length | 165 multiple-choice questions, three hours, closed book |
| Aids | Electronic calculator and notepad inside the exam interface only; no phones, tablets or cameras in the room |
| Scoring | Criterion-referenced cut score set by the modified Angoff method |
| Results | Preliminary results immediately on finishing and by email within 24 hours, reported as pass or fail; candidates who do **not** pass get scaled scores plus an "analysis of performance" (page 37) |
| 2026 fee | $410 member / $460 non-member; retake $355 / $405 (page 33) |
| 2027 fee | $425 member / $480 non-member; retake $370 / $425 (page 33) |
| Retakes | Wait 60 days; no more than three attempts in a two-year period, then a one-year wait |

ACI publishes no cut score for CRES, only the Angoff process and the scaled-score reporting.

Every lettered objective, in plain words:

**Anatomy & Physiology (7%)**
- A. Characteristics and functions of organs, with the heart, lungs, liver and brain named.
- B. Characteristics and functions of physiological systems, with gastrointestinal, circulatory, musculoskeletal and endocrine named.
- C. The relationship between anatomy, technique, and positioning, across all imaging modalities.
- D. Correct use of basic medical terminology.

**Public Safety in the Healthcare Facility (8%)**
- A. Electrical and mechanical safety protocols that protect patients, visitors and staff.
- B. Hazards of the magnetic environment around MRI, and electromagnetic interference from imaging equipment.
- C. Policies, procedures and physical activities that reduce the impact of radiation on patients, staff and visitors.
- D. Regulations, applicable standards and documentation requirements of the relevant accrediting organizations, with TJC, CMS, OSHA, FDA/BRH, MQSA, ACR and ANSI/AAMI standards named.
- E. Navigating and using the CMMS to document maintenance, track equipment history and recalls, and generate compliance and performance reports.

**Fundamentals of Electricity & Electronics (10%)**
- A. Foundational electronic theory applied to voltage, resistance, current and power.
- B. Fundamental theory of electronic components in a circuit.
- C. Facility power distribution, with 3-phase and 480 VAC named.
- D. Battery-powered systems, with ultrasound and portable x-ray named.
- E. Imaging system power distribution, with UPS, line conditioning and internal system distribution named.

**Healthcare Technology and Function (25%)**
- A. Functions of imaging system components, with x-ray tubes and generators, digital detectors, ultrasound probes and gamma cameras named.
- B. Systems and subsystems, their function, and how they are connected, including x-ray and ultrasound machines.
- C. Identifying and applying preventive and corrective maintenance procedures in accordance with regulatory standards.
- D. Correct use of positional terminology.
- E. Normal function, use and underlying technology of test equipment, with digital voltmeter, electrical safety analyzer and dosimeter named.

**Healthcare Technology Problem Solving (25%)**
- A. Prioritizing repairs by level of risk.
- B. Evaluating resources (schematic diagrams, manuals, diagnostic software, test equipment) to troubleshoot faulty imaging equipment.
- C. Troubleshooting and repairing imaging systems, with radiological, fluoroscopy, mammography and diagnostic ultrasound systems named.
- D. Telling a device error from a use error, including user training and applications, to decide the right action.
- E. Communicating effectively in speech and writing with clinicians, other hospital personnel and manufacturers about technical issues and projects.

**Healthcare Information Technology (25%)**
- A. Principles of system and network security and the actions needed for regulatory compliance.
- B. Fundamentals of hardware and software configuration as it applies to imaging systems, networking, communications and health information systems, with PACS, DICOM and Worklist named.
- C. Identifying and troubleshooting computer hardware and networking components, wired and wireless, with cable tracers, PING and DICOM echo named as the diagnostic tools.
- D. The interrelatedness of computer applications.

Worth noting what the outline does **not** name: CT and PET appear nowhere in it, and MRI appears only through the magnetic-environment safety objective. Our outline therefore teaches CT, MRI and nuclear medicine at recognition depth and spends its question budget on radiography, fluoroscopy, mammography and ultrasound, which the outline names explicitly.

## 3. CHTM: Certified Healthcare Technology Manager

### Scope (handbook page 20)

The healthcare technology manager plans and directs the activities of other HTM professionals, monitors their work, and takes corrective action. The handbook splits the credential into the management of healthcare technology operations and the management of personnel, and expects the holder to take part in the leadership of the business enterprise with strategic, business and change management skills plus employee relations.

Audience in plain words: the shop supervisor or HTM manager, not the bench technician. This is the first FieldReady pack whose reader does not fix anything.

### Eligibility (handbook pages 20 and 21)

ONE of five pathways. Every pathway that does not carry the supervisor or manager title requires confirmation of management duties by self-attestation or third-party attestation.

| # | Pathway |
|---|---|
| 1 | Current CCE, CBET, CRES or CLES certification, plus at least three years of work experience as an HTM supervisor or manager in the last five years |
| 2 | Completion of the Department of Defense BMET training program, plus at least three years of military or civilian experience as an HTM supervisor or manager in the last five years |
| 3 | Associate's degree in biomedical technology, a related healthcare discipline, information technology or business, plus at least three years as an HTM supervisor or manager in the last five years |
| 4 | Bachelor's degree or higher in biomedical technology, engineering, a related healthcare discipline, information technology or business, plus at least two years as an HTM manager within the last five years |
| 5 | Work experience with or without an unrelated degree: seven years in the HTM field, with three years of management experience in the last five years |

The handbook prints a preliminary list of accepted healthcare disciplines (accounting through technical writing) and invites candidates to email ACI if theirs is missing. Pathway 1 is the one our funnel cares about: a CBET or CRES holder with three years of supervision. That makes CHTM the natural upsell from the two technician packs, and it means a CHTM course page should link back to them.

**Financial Management (20%)**

**Operations Management (40%)**
- A. Manage the activities that deliver HTM service: work orders, project management, customer satisfaction.
- B. Ensure appropriate CMMS documentation regardless of who owns the equipment: asset inventory, parts, work orders, service history, recalls, end of life.
- C. Coordinate internal and external service provider activity: planning acquisition, contracting, installation, training, sustainment and lifecycle management.
- D. Review performance data to measure productivity and goal achievement: financials, activity reports, KPIs, staffing, competencies, cost reduction, program improvement.
- E. Develop department and team policies, procedures, goals and objectives: (1) maintain the policies and procedures, with MEMP, SOPs and the EOC manual named; (2) measure and report goals and objectives (KPIs, EOC metrics, compliance); (3) determine staffing and scheduling requirements; (4) report performance to the right committees and stakeholders, with EOC, patient safety, risk management, performance improvement, radiation safety, infection control, cost of service ratio and benchmarking named.
- F. Comply with federal, state and local regulatory requirements, accreditation requirements and deemed status organizations, with CMS, FDA, FCC, HIPAA and NRC named.
- G. Ensure resources are available to do the work: PPE, tools, test equipment, supplies, technical information and training.
- H. Participate in capital planning to evaluate technology requests: (1) assess project feasibility and requirements (space, site prep, power, network); (2) evaluate sustainability and service strategy (parts, training, service manuals, life expectancy, MAUDE reports); (3) advocate for standardization; (4) support oversight of cybersecurity and vulnerability (operating system life expectancy, network security, antivirus, patching).
- I. Engage in project management: create and review project plans, coordinate activities, engage stakeholders, implement the sustainment plan, join construction planning meetings.
- J. Lead department and team meetings and communications.
- K. Communicate with department leaders, vendors and stakeholders to encourage cooperation, identify issues and resolve problems: scheduled meetings, rounding, daily huddles.
- L. Collaborate with stakeholders (IT, nursing, vendors, informatics, technicians, senior leadership, facilities): (1) consult with users, management, vendors and technicians to assess technology needs; (2) collaborate with other departments on utility maintenance and interruption, including change management and downtime procedures; (3) maintain device and system interoperability.
- M. Ensure electronic data management of computerized assets: backups, security, patching, updates.

**Education & Training (11%)**
- A. Analyze training needs against cost, ROI, operations, technical requirements, competency, customer requirements, resources and asset inventory.
- B. Evaluate the effectiveness of training programs: cost, failure rates, total time to repair, staff competencies and skill set, user satisfaction.
- C. Manage training and professional development for staff: safety, soft skills, technical skills, regulatory and code compliance, internal department and facility policy, orientation and initial training (assign a mentor, SOPs, facility and shop).
- D. Facilitate operator training that addresses failure trending and use error.
- E. Collaborate with user departments and facilitate operator training for staff and inventory changes: database management, new equipment, new clinical applications, new staff.
- F. Collaborate with non-clinical departments: infection prevention, facilities management, materials management, IT.
- G. Assure the availability of training manuals: service manuals, operations manuals, training media and other educational material.

**Human Resources (13%)**
- A. Lead job description development, employee selection, retention and termination.
- B. Conduct performance evaluations of department and team staff: (1) evaluate performance to determine appropriate compensation; (2) evaluate observance of organizational values; (3) evaluate engagement; (4) develop performance development plans; (5) make appropriate recommendations to human resources.
- C. Perform personnel management activities: write and communicate periodic evaluations, document improvement opportunities, set employee goals, provide coaching and mentoring, deliver recognition, manage employee conflict, facilitate change, take part in personnel-related hearings and investigations on behalf of the organization, and ensure staff competency.
- D. Ensure department and team practice complies with federal, state and local labor law, with ADA, EEOC, FMLA and NLRA named.
- E. Ensure a safe work environment, with OSHA compliance and PPE utilization named.

## 4. Shared engine needs

**None beyond what BMET Plan A added.** Both packs are plain data in the existing shape. Specifically:

- Both use the standard exam-prep flow the engine already has: cheat sheet first, starter test, adaptive tutorial, 50-question practice tests, practice until three of the last five pass at 80 percent, then one official-format test at the vendor's score, recorded permanently.
- Both set `certificate: false`. The certificate view exists for BMET because CABT is an entry-level credential with no prerequisite; CRES and CHTM candidates are certifying for a real exam and do not need a completion certificate.
- Neither ships `FRA.labs`. The five virtual instruments are a BMET feature. A CRES pack could later add labs on a generator or detector panel, but that is a want, not a need, and nothing in the engine has to change to allow it.
- Untested domains are supported if we want a career or exam-day unit outside the weighted domains, as BMET domain 7 does.
- Pass bar: **70 percent**, following CBET. ACI reports a scaled score to CRES candidates who do not pass and publishes no cut score for either exam, so 70 is our assumption, stated as an assumption in the course copy exactly as the CBET pack does in `realExamNote`.

Draft manifest numbers, for whoever writes `course.js`:

| Field | CRES | CHTM |
|---|---|---|
| `exam` | vendor `AAMI / ACI`, code `CRES`, 165 questions, 180 minutes, `passPct: 70` | vendor `AAMI / ACI`, code `CHTM`, 100 questions, 120 minutes, `passPct: 70` |
| `test` | 50 questions, 55 minutes, pass 80, 3 of last 5 | 50 questions, 55 minutes, pass 80, 3 of last 5 |
| Domain quotas (sum 50) | 4 / 4 / 5 / 12 / 13 / 12 for A&P, Safety, Electricity, Function, Problem Solving, Healthcare IT | 10 / 8 / 20 / 6 / 6 for Financial, Risk, Operations, Education, HR |

The quota rows round the published weights to integers that sum to 50; Education and HR both land at 6 of 50, slightly flattening the 11 and 13 split, which is the same rounding the other packs do.

At five questions per lesson the CRES bank is about 300 and the CHTM bank about 250, which is six and five non-repeating 50-question tests respectively. CRES is comfortable on the minimum path; CHTM is at the same margin as the A+ and CBET packs, so the writer should aim for six questions on the heavier Operations lessons rather than five.

## 5. Order of work when we start

1. **CRES first.** It is the closer neighbour: three of its six domains come substantially from the CBET pack, five of the eight CBET generators transfer, and the reference bill is about $60. It also feeds CHTM, because CRES is one of the four certifications in CHTM eligibility pathway 1.
2. **CHTM second**, once CRES is live and the CRES audience exists to upsell. Its 44 percent of financial, education and HR content is entirely new writing and the least like anything we have shipped.

Each pack follows the standard pack workflow, unchanged:

- Buy and shelve the reference books first; nothing can be term-checked without them.
- Cheat sheet first, then units and lessons, deep dives, five or more questions per lesson, generators, acronym glossary, course page, catalog card.
- Original content only, no reference to any book or source anywhere in the pack, plain words, feedback buttons on every lesson.
- Plain ASCII in every pack file; write content files with the editor, never a shell heredoc.
- Term check every lesson against the books on disk, then the coverage check against the handbook outline so that every lettered objective is named by at least one lesson.
- Self-test green, `npm test` green, regenerate the static SEO pages, then stage with `scripts/stage.sh` and hand Blake the deploy command. Blake pushes.
